# Sentence Patterns & Grammar Notes

---

## 1. "When describing" vs "When you describe" vs "When we describe"

**Original sentence:** *When describing an outcome that wasn't planned or was unexpected — often after a chain of events. One of the most natural storytelling phrases in English.*

| Form | Effect |
|------|--------|
| When **describing** an outcome | General — applies to anyone; subject is implied |
| When **you describe** an outcome | Directly addresses the reader |
| When **we describe** an outcome | Includes both writer and reader |

> "When describing" is essentially saying "When [you are] describing..." — the subject is dropped for a more universal, formal tone.

---

## 2. "Is using" vs "Uses" — Present Continuous vs Simple Present

**Original sentence:** *"This part is describing what 'those days' are like in general — as a type of day everyone recognizes."*

| Form | Effect |
|------|--------|
| "The author **is using** this word to suggest..." | Focuses on the act — feels immediate, dynamic |
| "The author **uses** this word to suggest..." | States a general fact — more neutral and formal |

Both are correct and used interchangeably in grammar and literary analysis.

---

## 3. "Seems" vs "Seemed" — Present vs Past Tense After "One of Those Days"

**Original sentence:** *Yesterday was one of those days when everything seems to go wrong at once.*

"One of those days" refers to a universally recognized *type* of day, not yesterday specifically. General truths use present tense:

| Form | Effect |
|------|--------|
| **seems** | Universal — this is how that type of day always feels |
| **seemed** | Specific — focuses on yesterday's particular experience; more literal, less idiomatic |

---

## 4. "Could Have Written" / "Was a Stylistic Choice" — Why Past Tense?

**Original sentence:** *"I could have written 'this part describes' and it would be perfectly correct. The 'is describing' framing was a stylistic choice that made the analysis feel more dynamic, not a grammatical requirement."*

Both phrases are anchored to a specific past moment (when the writing happened):

- **"could have written"** — past modal; the option existed back then but wasn't taken. "Can write" would wrongly imply the option is still open now.
- **"was a stylistic choice"** — describes a completed past decision. "Is" would imply a timeless general principle.

---

## 5. "When Making" vs "When Make" — Why the -ing Form Is Required

**Original sentence:** *Always keep the consumer in mind when making decisions.*

After "when," a verb must be properly anchored. You cannot use a bare verb:

| Form | Status |
|------|--------|
| "when you **make** decisions" | ✅ Full clause — anchored by subject |
| "when **making** decisions" | ✅ -ing phrase — anchored by -ing form |
| "when **make** decisions" | ❌ Bare verb — nothing connects it to "when" |

The same rule applies to other conjunctions:

| ✅ Correct | ❌ Incorrect |
|-----------|------------|
| while working | while work |
| before leaving | before leave |

> The -ing form is the *minimum grammatical requirement* when dropping the subject — not optional decoration.

---

## 6. "Where" vs "When" — Relative Adverbs for Time Expressions

**Original sentence:** *You want to describe a day where nothing worked out.*

| Form | Effect |
|------|--------|
| "a day **when** nothing worked out" | Technically precise — "when" is correct for time |
| "a day **where** nothing worked out" | Colloquial — treats the day as a context or space |

"Where" has expanded beyond physical place in everyday English to mean "a situation in which":
- "a point **where** I gave up"
- "a stage **where** growth slows down"

In casual conversation, **"where" often sounds more natural** to native speakers, though "when" is preferred in formal writing.

## 7. The more… the more…

**Original sentence:** *The more you practice, the better you get.*

This is a **parallel comparative structure** showing that two things change in proportion to each other.

**Basic structure:**
> The + comparative + subject + verb, the + comparative + subject + verb

**How it works:** "The" here is not the definite article — it's an old English adverb meaning "by that much." So *"the more you practice, the better you get"* means: *by how much more you practice, by that much you get better.* It's essentially a compressed causal clause: *As you practice more, you get progressively better.*

**What can fill each slot:**

| Slot | Examples |
|------|---------|
| Adjective | the more **careful**, the more **expensive** |
| Adverb | the more **quickly**, the more **often** |
| Noun phrase | the more **time** you spend, the more **money** you earn |
| Verb alone | the more you **eat**, the more you **want** |

**Key rules:**
- Both sides must be parallel — comparative on both sides
- You cannot drop "the": ❌ *More you eat, more you want*
- The comparative moves to the front: *The more carefully **you read*** (not *The more you carefully read*)

**Variations:**
- With "less": *The less you worry, the happier you'll be.*
- Mixed: *The more you spend, the less you save.*

## 8. "Speak up" vs "Speak" — Phrasal Verbs

**Original sentence:** *Why didn't you speak up in the meeting?*

**"Speak"** = the basic act of using your voice.
**"Speak up"** = express your opinion, raise a concern, or say something when you had the opportunity to stay silent.

The question is not asking *why you didn't make sounds* — it's asking: *why didn't you share your opinion when you had the chance?*

**How "up" changes the meaning:**

| Phrase | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| speak | use your voice |
| speak up | voice your opinion; take a stand |
| stand | be on your feet |
| stand up | rise; or defend someone |
| give | hand something over |
| give up | quit; surrender |

**"Speak up" has two meanings depending on context:**
- *"Why didn't you speak up in the meeting?"* → share your opinion
- *"Could you speak up? I can't hear you."* → speak louder

The choice of "speak up" over "speak" signals the speaker is asking about **courage or initiative** — why you stayed silent when you could have contributed.

## 9. "Due" — Meaning and Usage

**Original sentence:** *I have a presentation due on Monday.*

**"Due"** is an adjective meaning **scheduled to be submitted or completed by a deadline**.

*"I have a presentation due on Monday"* = my presentation must be ready/submitted by Monday.

**Common meanings of "due":**

| Usage | Meaning | Example |
|-------|---------|---------|
| Deadline | must be done/submitted by | *The essay is due Monday.* |
| Expected arrival | scheduled to arrive | *The train is due at 9am.* |
| Owed | something you're entitled to | *Credit is due to the whole team.* |
| Due to | because of | *The delay was due to bad weather.* |

**"Due" vs "by":**
- *"The presentation is **due** on Monday"* — states the deadline as a fact about the task
- *"Submit the presentation **by** Monday"* — gives a direct instruction about timing

Both mean the same thing in practice, but "due" is about the **status of the task**, while "by" is about the **time constraint**.

## 10. Singular vs Plural After "There is no" — "There's no universal answer"

**Original sentence:** *There's no universal answer.*

The singular is used because the sentence negates the existence of **one concept** — a single universal answer. This follows the pattern:

> **There is no + singular noun** = this thing does not exist (as a concept)

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| *There is no universal **answer*** | A definitive, one-size-fits-all answer simply doesn't exist |
| *There are no universal **answers*** | Multiple potential answers all fail to be universal |

Both are grammatically correct, but the singular emphasizes the **absence of one ideal thing**, while the plural emphasizes the **absence of a set of things**.

**Same pattern:**
- *There is no time.*
- *There is no easy solution.*
- *There is no shortcut.*

## 11. Conventionally Plural Nouns — "It depends on your circumstances"

**Original sentence:** *It really depends on your circumstances.*

"Circumstances" is almost always used in the **plural** in everyday English, because it inherently implies a **collection of interconnected factors and conditions**.

| Form | Status |
|------|--------|
| *your **circumstances*** | Natural and standard |
| *your **circumstance*** | Sounds unnatural in most contexts |

The singular *circumstance* survives only in formal or literary phrases:
- *by force of circumstance*
- *a circumstance beyond our control*

**Other nouns that are conventionally plural:**

| Word | Notes |
|------|-------|
| surroundings | not "surrounding" |
| belongings | not "belonging" |
| savings | not "saving" (when referring to money) |
| proceedings | not "proceeding" in legal contexts |

These words have become **lexically plural** — the plural is the default form, not a count of individual items.

| Word | Example |

| regards | *She sends her regards.* |
| congratulations | *Congratulations on the new job!* |
| condolences | *Please accept my condolences.* |

**Money & Resources**

| Word     | Example                           |

## 13. "Kind of" vs "Kinds of" — Singular vs Plural

**Original sentences:**
- *What kinds of jobs do young people like to do?*
- *What kind of people earn the highest salaries?*

The number of *kind/kinds* follows how much **variety or diversity** you're implying.

| Sentence | Why |
|----------|-----|
| *What **kinds** of jobs...* | Many different job categories expected — plural |
| *What **kind** of people...* | One profile or type in mind — singular |

**The rule:**

- Use **kinds** when you expect **multiple distinct categories**
- Use **kind** when you're thinking of **one type or profile**

**More examples:**

| Sentence | Why |
|----------|-----|
| *What kinds of food do you like?* | Many different food categories expected |
| *What kind of person is she?* | Asking about one character profile |
| *What kinds of music do teenagers listen to?* | Multiple genres expected |
| *What kind of job suits me?* | Looking for one best-fit type |

**Note:** In casual spoken English, native speakers often use *kind* and *kinds* interchangeably — the distinction is subtle and more about precision than strict grammar.

## 14. "At" vs "In" with Companies and Organizations

**Original sentences:**
- *Software engineers at major companies earn the highest salaries.*
- *Senior executives in large corporations also earn enormous amounts.*

Both "at" and "in" can be used with companies, but they carry a slightly different meaning.

| Preposition | When to use | Example |
|-------------|------------|---------|
| **at** | referring to where someone works — the organization as employer | *She works at Google.* |
| **in** | referring to someone's position or role within an organization | *He's in a large corporation's IT department.* |

**The key distinction:**
- **"at"** → focuses on the **employer** (where you work)
- **"in"** → focuses on the **internal structure or role** (your place within it)

**More examples:**

| Sentence | Why |
|----------|-----|
| *Software engineers **at** major tech companies* | "at" — referring to the employer |
| *Senior executives **in** large corporations* | "in" — referring to their position within the org |
| *She works **at** a startup.* | "at" — the company as a place of work |
| *He's **in** finance.* | "in" — the industry/field they operate in |

**Note:** In casual spoken English, "at" and "in" are often used interchangeably with companies and most native speakers wouldn't notice the difference. The distinction is subtle and more relevant in writing.

## 15. Professional, Specialist, Expert and Related Words

These words all relate to expertise but differ in meaning and usage.

**Professional** (noun/adjective) — someone who does something as their career or paid occupation, often requiring formal training or qualifications.
- *She is a medical professional.*
- *He gave a very professional presentation.*

**Specialist** (noun) — someone who focuses on one specific area within a broader field.
- *She's a specialist in tax law.*
- *I was referred to a heart specialist.*

**Expert** (noun/adjective) — someone with a high level of skill or knowledge, gained through experience or study. Less formal than specialist.
- *He's an expert in cybersecurity.*
- *She gave expert advice.*

**Skilled worker / Skilled professional** — emphasizes practical hands-on ability rather than academic knowledge.
- *The country needs more skilled workers in construction.*

**Technician** (noun) — someone with technical, practical expertise, often in engineering, IT, or science.
- *An IT technician fixed the server.*

**Practitioner** (noun) — someone who actively practises a profession, especially medicine or law.
- *A general practitioner (GP) is a family doctor.*
- *Legal practitioners must pass the bar exam.*

**Specialized** (adjective) — describes a skill, field, knowledge, or equipment that is highly specific and focused, not general. It describes the *thing*, not the person.
- *She has specialized knowledge in neuroscience.*
- *This requires specialized training.*
- *He works in a highly specialized field.*

**The "specialize" word family:**

| Form | Example |
|------|---------|
| specialize (verb) | *He specializes in data science.* |
| specialist (noun) | *She's a tax specialist.* |
| specialized (adjective) | *This requires specialized training.* |
| specialization (noun) | *His specialization is neurology.* |

**How they compare:**

| Word | Type | Describes |
|------|------|-----------|
| specialized | adjective | a skill, field, knowledge, equipment |
| specialist | noun | a person |
| specialize | verb | what a person does |

| Word | Focus | Typical fields |
|------|-------|---------------|
| professional | paid career, qualified | any field |
| specialist | narrow focus within a field | medicine, law, finance |
| expert | deep knowledge or skill | any field |
| technician | practical/technical skills | IT, engineering, science |
| practitioner | active practice of a profession | medicine, law, therapy |

## 16. Word Families — Attentively, Promptly, Genuine, Empathy

These words appeared in the sentence: *A good customer service experience involves someone who listens attentively, responds promptly, and shows genuine empathy rather than just reading from a script.*

**Attentively** (adverb) — giving full focus and care to what someone is saying or doing.
- *She listened attentively during the meeting.*
- *The nurse attentively monitored the patient.*

**Promptly** (adverb) — doing something immediately, without delay.
- *He responded promptly to the email.*
- *The ambulance arrived promptly.*

**Genuine** (adjective) — real, sincere, not fake or pretended.
- *She showed genuine concern for her customers.*
- *His apology didn't feel genuine.*

**Empathy** (noun) — the ability to understand and share another person's feelings; to put yourself in their shoes.
- *Good doctors need empathy.*
- *She showed great empathy towards the grieving family.*

**Word family summary:**

| Base | Adjective | Adverb | Noun |
|------|-----------|--------|------|
| attend | attentive | attentively | attention |
| prompt | prompt | promptly | promptness |
| genuine | genuine | genuinely | genuineness |
| empathy | empathetic | empathetically | empathy |

## 17. The Preposition "Through" — Meanings and Usage

**Original sentence:** *It's a philosophy that runs through everything they do.*

"Through" is a versatile preposition with several distinct meanings:

**1. Physical movement — from one side to the other**
- *She walked through the door.*
- *The train went through the tunnel.*

**2. Duration — across an entire period of time**
- *He worked through the night.*
- *She slept through the whole film.*

**3. By means of / via**
- *I got the job through a friend.*
- *We communicate through email.*

**4. Permeating or affecting every part of something** ← meaning in the original sentence
- *It's a philosophy that runs through everything they do.*
- *Fear ran through the crowd.*
- *A theme of loss runs through the entire novel.*

**5. Because of / as a result of**
- *She succeeded through hard work.*
- *He failed through carelessness.*

**Summary:**

| Meaning | Example |
|---------|---------|
| physical passage | *walk through the door* |
| entire time period | *worked through the night* |
| by means of | *heard through a friend* |
| permeating everything | *runs through everything they do* |
| because of | *succeeded through effort* |

## 18. Phrasal Verb — "Come Through"

**"Come through"** has several meanings depending on context:

**1. To succeed or deliver when needed — the most common meaning**
- *I knew you'd come through for me.*
- *She always comes through under pressure.*
- *The team came through in the final moment.*

**2. To arrive or be received**
- *Your test results have come through.*
- *The payment finally came through.*
- *Has the visa come through yet?*

**3. To survive or get through a difficult experience**
- *He came through the surgery well.*
- *She came through a really tough year.*

**4. To be clearly communicated or felt**
- *Your passion really comes through in your writing.*
- *His nervousness came through during the presentation.*

**Summary:**

| Meaning | Example |
|---------|---------|
| succeed/deliver when needed | *She always comes through.* |
| arrive/be received | *The results came through.* |
| survive something difficult | *He came through the crisis.* |
| be clearly felt/visible | *Your enthusiasm comes through.* |

## 20. "There's a lot" — Why Singular?

**Original sentence:** *There's a lot to weigh up, honestly.*

**"There's"** = **"there is"** — singular because the subject is **"a lot"**, which is grammatically singular in English, even though it refers to many things. "A lot" is treated as one mass or quantity, not as individual countable items.

| Sentence | Why singular |
|----------|-------------|
| *There's a lot to consider.* | "a lot" = one quantity → singular |
| *There's a lot of work to do.* | same pattern |
| *There's a lot of noise outside.* | same pattern |

**Compare with plural:**
When "a lot" is replaced with countable items, it switches to plural:
- *There **are** many things to weigh up.* ✅
- *There **are** lots of factors to consider.* ✅

Note that **"lots of"** (with an *s*) tends to use plural:
- *There **are** lots of things to think about.* ✅

**The simple rule:**

| Form | Number | Example |
|------|--------|---------|
| *a lot* | singular → *there **is** a lot* | *There's a lot to think about.* |
| *lots of things / many things* | plural → *there **are*** | *There are lots of things to think about.* |

## 21. "A lot of" vs "Lots of"

Both mean "a large amount or number of something" and are largely interchangeable, but with subtle differences:

| | a lot of | lots of |
|--|---------|---------|
| Register | neutral — works in formal and informal | more informal and conversational |
| Feel | slightly more measured | slightly more emphatic or enthusiastic |
| Written English | more common in writing | less common in formal writing |

**Examples showing the difference in feel:**
- *She has a lot of experience.* — neutral, professional
- *She has lots of experience!* — warmer, more enthusiastic

**Grammar — verb agreement:**
- *There **is** a lot of noise.* — singular verb ✅
- *There **are** lots of people.* — plural verb ✅
- *There **are** a lot of people.* — also acceptable ✅

**The simple takeaway:** In conversation they are interchangeable. *"Lots of"* sounds slightly more casual and enthusiastic; *"a lot of"* is safer in formal or written English.

## 22. Present Simple + Past Perfect in the Same Sentence

**Original sentence:** *Often people come back with fresh perspectives on problems they'd been stuck on.*

This sentence uses **two different tenses** doing two different jobs:

**"come" — present simple**
Used because of the word *"often"* — the sentence is making a **general statement about a recurring pattern**, not describing one specific past event.
- *Often people **come** back...* = this is what tends to happen in general

**"they'd been stuck on" — past perfect (had been)**
Used to describe the **background condition** that happened earlier in the past — being stuck came before coming back with fresh perspectives.

**Timeline:**
> stuck on problems (earlier) → came back with fresh perspectives (later)

*had been stuck* = the struggle happened first, in the background, before the fresh perspective arrived.

**The two tenses co-exist because they do different jobs:**

| Part | Tense | Job |
|------|-------|-----|
| *people come back* | present simple | general recurring truth |
| *they'd been stuck on* | past perfect | background condition that happened earlier |

**Compare:**
- *Often people **come** back with fresh perspectives.* — general truth, always true
- *People **came** back with fresh perspectives.* — one specific past event

**Similar patterns using present simple for general truths:**
- *Often, taking a break **helps** you think more clearly.*
- *Sometimes stepping away **gives** you a new angle.*
- *People **learn** best when they're relaxed.*

## 23. Understanding "Up" in Phrasal Verbs

"Up" rarely means physical direction in phrasal verbs. It usually carries one of five core ideas — once you understand the logic, the collocations become much easier to remember.

---

**1. Up = completion or totality** — something is fully done or used up

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| eat up | finish all the food |
| drink up | finish your drink |
| use up | exhaust the supply |
| fill up | make completely full |
| clean up | make fully clean |

---

**2. Up = creation or production** — bringing something into existence

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| think up | create an idea in your mind |
| come up with | produce an idea or solution |
| make up | invent a story or excuse |
| draw up | produce a document or plan |
| build up | create something gradually |

---

**3. Up = increase or intensity** — something grows or gets stronger

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| speak up | speak louder |
| turn up | increase the volume |
| speed up | go faster |
| heat up | become hotter |
| step up | increase effort or pace |

---

**4. Up = surfacing or appearing** — something comes to attention

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| come up | a topic arises in conversation |
| show up | appear or arrive |
| bring up | raise a topic |
| turn up | appear unexpectedly |
| crop up | arise unexpectedly |

---

**5. Up = lifting or raising** — both physical and metaphorical

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| lift up | physically raise |
| cheer up | raise someone's mood |
| pick up | lift from the ground / learn a skill |
| hold up | keep raised / delay something |

---

**The trick to remembering:**

When you see a phrasal verb with "up", ask: *which of these five ideas does it fit?*

| Phrasal verb | Category | Logic |
