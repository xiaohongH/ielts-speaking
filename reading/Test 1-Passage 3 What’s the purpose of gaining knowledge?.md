**27**

A I would ==found an institution== where any person can find instruction in any subject.' That was the founder's ==motto== for Cornell University, and it seems ==an apt characterization== of the different university, also in the USA, where I currently teach philosophy. A student can prepare for a career in resort management, engineering, interior design, accounting, music, law enforcement, ==you name it==. But what would the founders of these two institutions have thought of a course called ==Arson for Profit==? I kid you not: we have it ==on the books==. Any ==undergraduates== who have ==met the academic requirements== can sign up for the course in our program in 'fire science'.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph A
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **found** (v.) = to establish / create an institution. ⚠️ Not "find"! Note the pun: *"found an institution where any person can **find** instruction."*
> - **motto** (n.) = a short phrase expressing an ideal or guiding belief.
> - **apt characterization** = a suitable / fitting description. (*apt* = appropriate)
> - **you name it** (idiom) = and anything else you can think of.
> - **on the books** (idiom) = officially exists / is officially recorded.
> - **undergraduate** (n.) = a student studying for a first (bachelor's) degree.
> - **met the academic requirements** = satisfied the necessary conditions to enrol.
> - **arson** (n.) = the crime of deliberately setting fire to property.
>
> **Main idea:** The author teaches philosophy at a US university that offers a huge range of vocational courses — including, surprisingly, one nicknamed *"Arson for Profit"* within its "fire science" program, open to any qualified undergraduate.

**28**

B Naturally, the course is intended for ==prospective== arson investigators, who can learn all the ==tricks of the trade== for detecting whether a fire was ==deliberately set==, discovering who did it, and establishing a ==chain of evidence== for effective ==prosecution== in a ==court of law==. But wouldn't this also be the perfect course for prospective ==arsonists== to sign up for? My point is not to criticize academic programs in fire science: they are highly welcome as part of the increasing ==professionalization== of this and many other ==occupations==. However, it's not unknown for a firefighter to ==torch== a building. This example suggests how dishonest and illegal behavior, with the help of higher education, can ==creep into== every aspect of public and business life.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph B
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **prospective** (adj.) = likely to become; future (*prospective investigators / arsonists*).
> - **tricks of the trade** (idiom) = the clever skills / techniques of a particular job.
> - **deliberately set** = started on purpose (opposite: accidental).
> - **chain of evidence** = a connected sequence of proof.
> - **prosecution** (n.) = the act of taking legal action against someone in court.
> - **court of law** = the place where legal cases are judged.
> - **professionalization** (n.) = the process of an occupation becoming more professional / regulated.
> - **occupation** (n.) = a job / profession.
> - **torch** (v.) = to set fire to something deliberately (informal).
> - **creep into** (phrasal v.) = to gradually enter, usually something unwanted or bad.
>
> **Main idea:** The course is designed for future arson *investigators*, but the same knowledge would suit future arson*ists*. The author isn't criticizing fire science — he's showing how dishonest behaviour can quietly enter every part of life *with the help of education*.

**29**

C I realized this ==anew== when I was invited to speak before a class in marketing, which is another of our degree programs. The regular instructor is a colleague who appreciates the kind of ==ethical perspective== I can bring as a philosopher. There are endless ways I could have approached this assignment, but I ==took my cue from== the title of the course: 'Principles of Marketing'. It made me think to ask the students, =='Is marketing principled?'== After all, a subject matter can have principles in the sense of being ==codified==, having rules, as with football or chess, without being ==principled== in the sense of being ethical. Many of the students immediately assumed that the answer to my question about marketing principles was obvious: no. Just look at the ways in which ==everything under the sun== has been marketed; obviously it need not be done in a principled (=ethical) fashion.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph C
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **anew** (adv.) = again, in a new way.
> - **ethical perspective** = a moral point of view. (*ethical* = concerning right and wrong)
> - **take one's cue from** (idiom) = to take guidance / inspiration from something.
> - **codified** (adj./v.) = arranged into an organised system of rules.
> - ⚠️ **Key distinction:** *having principles* (rules, like chess) ≠ *being principled* (being ethical / moral).
> - **everything under the sun** (idiom) = everything that exists; absolutely everything.
> - **in a … fashion** = in a … way / manner.
>
> **Main idea:** Speaking to a marketing class, the author asks *"Is marketing principled?"* He separates two meanings of "principle": having **rules** (codified) vs. being **ethical** (principled). Students assumed marketing clearly is *not* ethical.

**30**

D Is that obvious? I made the suggestion, which may sound ==downright crazy== ==in light of== the evidence, that perhaps marketing is ==by definition== principled. My inspiration for this judgement is the philosopher ==Immanuel Kant==, who argued that any body of knowledge consists of ==an end (or purpose) and a means==.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph D
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **downright** (adv.) = completely, utterly (used for emphasis: *downright crazy / rude*).
> - **in light of** = considering / taking into account.
> - **by definition** = by its very nature; necessarily.
> - **a body of knowledge** = an organised field of information / study.
> - ⭐ **means vs. end** — Kant's key idea:
>   - **means** = the *how* (methods / techniques).
>   - **end** = the *why* (purpose / goal).
>
> **Main idea:** The author proposes the opposite of the students' view — that marketing is **by definition principled**. His basis is Kant's claim that any field of knowledge has both a **means** and an **end**.

**31**

E Let us apply both the terms 'means' and 'end' to marketing. The students have signed up for a course in order to learn how to market effectively. But ==to what end==? There seem to be two main attitudes toward that question. One is that the answer is obvious: the purpose of marketing is to sell things and to make money. The other attitude is that the purpose of marketing is ==irrelevant==: Each person comes to the program and course with his or her own plans, and these need not even concern the ==acquisition of marketing expertise== as such. My proposal, which I believe would also be Kant's, is that neither of these attitudes ==captures the significance== of the end to the means for marketing. ==A field of knowledge or a professional endeavor is defined by both the means and the end; hence both deserve scrutiny.== Students need to study both how to achieve X, and also what X is.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph E
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **to what end?** = for what purpose?
> - **irrelevant** (adj.) = not important / not connected to the matter.
> - **acquisition** (n.) = the act of gaining or obtaining something (*acquisition of expertise*).
> - **expertise** (n.) = expert skill or knowledge.
> - **capture the significance** = to express / grasp the importance of something.
> - **endeavor** (n.) = a serious effort or attempt (*a professional endeavor*).
> - **hence** (adv.) = therefore, for this reason.
> - **scrutiny** (n.) = careful and detailed examination. → *deserve scrutiny* = deserve close examination.
>
> **⭐ Sentence to remember:** *"A field of knowledge or a professional endeavor is defined by both the means and the end; hence both deserve scrutiny."*
>
> **Main idea:** Applying means/end to marketing: students learn *how* to market (means), but *to what end?* Two common views — (1) the purpose is obvious (sell / make money); (2) the purpose is irrelevant (everyone has their own plans). The author argues both views miss the point: a field is defined by **both** means and end, so **both** need examining.

**32**

F It is at this point that 'Arson for Profit' becomes ==supremely relevant==. That course is presumably all about means: how to detect and prosecute criminal activity. It is therefore assumed that the end is good in an ethical sense. When I ask fire science students to ==articulate== the end, or purpose, of their field, they eventually ==generalize to== something like, 'The ==safety and welfare== of society,' which seems right. As we have seen, someone could use the very same knowledge of means to achieve a much less ==noble end==, such as personal profit via destructive, dangerous, ==reckless== activity. But we would not call that firefighting. We have a separate word for it: arson. Similarly, if you employed the 'principles of marketing' in an unprincipled way, you would not be doing marketing. We have another term for it: ==fraud==. Kant gives the example of a doctor and a poisoner, who use the ==identical== knowledge to achieve their ==divergent ends==. We would say that one is practicing medicine, the other, murder.

> [!tip] 📝 Notes — Paragraph F
> **Vocabulary & phrases**
> - **supremely relevant** = extremely relevant. (*supremely* = to the highest degree)
> - **articulate** (v.) = to express (an idea) clearly in words.
> - **generalize to** = to move from specifics to a broad statement.
> - **welfare** (n.) = the health, happiness, and well-being of a person or group.
> - **noble end** = a morally good / honourable purpose. (*noble* = admirable, high-minded)
> - **reckless** (adj.) = careless about danger or consequences.
> - **fraud** (n.) = the crime of deceiving people for financial gain.
> - **identical** (adj.) = exactly the same.
> - **divergent ends** = different / opposing purposes. (*divergent* = moving apart)
>
> **⭐ Core analogy (same means → different ends):**
> | Same knowledge (means) | Good end | Bad end |
> |---|---|---|
> | Fire science | firefighting | **arson** |
> | Marketing principles | marketing | **fraud** |
> | Medical knowledge | medicine (doctor) | **murder** (poisoner) |
>
> **Main idea:** *"Arson for Profit"* teaches means and assumes a good end — *"the safety and welfare of society."* But the same means used for a bad end isn't firefighting; it's **arson**. Likewise, unprincipled marketing is **fraud**, and medical knowledge misused is **murder**. So the **end** defines what the field truly is — supporting the author's claim that marketing, properly practised, is *by definition* principled.

## 🧠 Whole-passage summary

The author (a philosophy professor) uses a provocatively-named course, *"Arson for Profit,"* to make a Kantian point: **every field of knowledge is defined by both a *means* (how) and an *end* (why).** The *same* skills can serve a good end or a bad one — firefighting vs. arson, medicine vs. murder, marketing vs. fraud. Therefore a discipline can't be judged by its techniques alone; its **purpose** is essential. His surprising conclusion: genuine marketing is, *by definition*, ethical ("principled") — because using its methods unethically turns it into something else entirely (fraud).
